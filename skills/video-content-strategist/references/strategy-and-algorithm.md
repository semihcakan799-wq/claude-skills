# Strategy and Algorithm

What to make, for whom, on which platform, in what mix, and how to measure it. These principles hold across Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, LinkedIn, and Facebook because every feed is built on the same business model.

**When to use:** planning a channel or content calendar, choosing formats or platforms, diagnosing "views but no leads" or "no views at all", setting monetization strategy.

**When not to use:** the plan is set and the user needs a specific script (load `script-structures.md`).

---

## 1. How Feed Algorithms Work

Platforms earn money from ads, ads scale with user minutes, so the algorithm is a **matchmaker**: it shows each viewer the content most likely to keep them on the platform.

1. **Digital fingerprint:** on upload, the video is analyzed multimodally (computer vision on visuals, audio and transcript, caption, hashtags, creator history) into a topic map.
2. **Fit score:** a prediction of who will like it.
3. **Sample test:** shown to a small group (roughly a few hundred), mostly non-followers, because strangers liking it is the real signal.
4. **Decision:** positive data pushes to an order of magnitude more people, then again, until it saturates; neutral data re-samples a new small group; negative data stops distribution quickly ("view jail").

**Signals that decide the sample result**
- Average watch time and percent completion
- Engagement rate: (likes + comments + shares) / views
- Watch-time session share: how much of the viewer's session went to your videos (not visible to creators)
- On YouTube specifically: click-through rate on the thumbnail plus average view duration

**Two levers for creators:**
1. Help the algorithm build a better fit score: **audience matching** (below).
2. Make the sample engage: relevant topic, non-obvious and implementable information, high comprehension, short distance to results.

**What does not matter much:** posting time, hashtag tricks, caption hacks. Great videos for one avatar on a narrow band of topics is the cake; everything else is icing. The words you say and write do matter, because the transcript and caption are how the algorithm detects the topic, so say your topic's words out loud.

## 2. Audience Matching

Make every video on an account for the **same ideal viewer**, around the **same few topics**. After 30-50 consistent videos the algorithm knows exactly whom to sample, and growth compounds.

- Start with the audience, not the topic: picture one dream viewer and their problems.
- Experiment freely with **formats** (the shape of the dart); never with **audience** (the board). Every off-target dart shrinks the board.
- Mixed topics produce blended fit scores, weak samples, and flops. One off-avatar viral hit can damage the next several videos.
- Audit: scroll your last 20 videos. Same viewer every time? If yes and views are still low, the problem is video quality. If no, the problem is targeting.
- The same video can do 1M views on a well-matched channel and under 2,000 on a mismatched one.

**People watch for themselves, not for you.** They like the messenger but come for the message.

## 3. Two Games: Awareness vs Conversion

Pick one game per account. Running both is possible only on separate accounts.

| | Awareness game | Conversion game |
|---|---------------|-----------------|
| Creator type | Entertainment | Education |
| Goal | Maximum views in the category | Maximum on-target views and leads |
| Monetization | Ad revenue share, brand deals | Own products, services, affiliates |
| Players that matter | Algorithm, passive viewers, other entertainment creators, brands | Algorithm, active viewers (buyers), other creators of both kinds |
| Content attributes | Shareworthy, curiosity hook, **emotional payoff at the end**, **large total addressable market** (broad references) | On-target shareworthy, large **on-target** market, problem-solution clarity, **tactical, non-obvious, immediately implementable** solution |
| Feel | More dopamine, volatile | More money, stable |

**Walk-away value test (conversion):** could the viewer walk away, make one change, and see an improvement? If not, the video is entertainment in disguise.

**Players and incentives:** the algorithm wants user minutes; passive viewers want dopamine (emotion, curiosity); active viewers want the fastest implementable relief from a pain; entertainment creators want views; education creators want leads; brands want return on ad spend. Give each player what it wants and it pushes your content.

## 4. Virality Is a Trap (On-Target Virality)

**Pure virality** goes as broad as possible and reaches mixed audiences who will never buy, confusing the algorithm about whom to target next. **On-target virality** saturates a narrow slice of your buyers and nobody else. Algorithms favor precision; cult favorites in a niche are strong matches ("the riches are in the niches").

Filter for every idea: **Will my core viewer find this interesting, useful, valuable, or entertaining?** If not, do not make it, even if it would go viral.

## 5. Share, Save, and Comment Psychology

**Shares drive views.** People share for social or professional **credit** from the recipient (being seen as funny, smart, caring, tapped in). Credit only flows if the recipient has not seen it, so be **first** or **different**. A share by another creator to their audience is a **super share**, worth thousands of ordinary shares; it requires ideas novel enough that a peer gains credit by reposting them.

**Saves drive follows** (both mean "I want more of this later"). Five ways to earn saves:
1. Clear outcome signaling ("this will help you get X")
2. Lots of tactical information on screen (lists, tutorials, breakdowns)
3. Deliberately more information than can be remembered in one viewing
4. Specific inputs shown on screen to reuse later (templates, settings, prompts, scripts)
5. Outcome reminders throughout

**Comments drive distribution.** Five drivers: take a hard stance; take the contrarian side; amplify the framing ("better than every restaurant in the city" beats "a good recipe"); build around cult-loved brands, people, or ideas that viewers already have opinions on; trigger emotion. Keyword CTAs and "what should I cover next?" prompts also raise comments.

## 6. Format Strategy

### Format pyramid (saturation ladder)

| Level | Format family | Examples |
|-------|--------------|----------|
| 4 (top) | **Stories** | Client conversations, day in the life, personal epiphany: insight only you could have |
| 3 | **Application** | Scenarios, hypotheticals, live case studies, challenges, live builds: you prove your skill on camera |
| 2 | **Explanation** | Breakdowns, tutorials, problem-solution: facts plus your opinion and analysis |
| 1 (base) | **Facts** | Listicles, rankings, comparisons |

In unsaturated niches all levels work (and lower levels reach bigger audiences). As a niche saturates, a red line rises from the bottom: only formats above it outperform unless you are the best at a lower one. Climb the pyramid as competition grows.

**Content types, current era:** entertainment; storytelling (entertaining stories wrapped around a lesson); **application** (the newest opportunity: demonstrate expertise inside the video); tactical education (still works if the topic is novel); and **eduslop** (listing facts with no insight or opinion: stopped working).

### Six storytelling formats for personal brands
1. Challenge: a large outcome in a short time, showing how
2. Scenario or hypothetical: "If I had to grow a [business] from 0, here's exactly what I'd do"
3. Hero's journey: desired outcome, blocked by a common problem, solved by a novel approach
4. Live breakdown: a client build, audit, or mentoring session
5. Personal learning or epiphany: a problem solved by a method you discovered
6. Social show: episodic series with recurring characters or segments

### Six formats for "boring" businesses
1. Case study project: retroactively walk through a finished job
2. Live work execution: voice-over while doing the work
3. Scenario build: walk through a hypothetical project (renders, plans, schematics)
4. Show the factory: behind the scenes of production
5. Extremes: the most extreme example (the dirtiest deck, the biggest door)
6. S-tier ranking: rank methods, materials, or brands with nuance that proves expertise

**Whiteboard explainer:** teacher, handheld, or split-screen layout; start with something covered or blocked on the board (a built-in curiosity loop) while the text hook stays visible; reveal progressively; pre-write the points to keep speed.

## 7. Planning Systems

### 3-3-1 strategy (10 videos in 10 minutes)
1. Write the niche in the center of a page; pick the **3 subtopics** your viewer cares about most (check what already performs for others).
2. Pick **3 formats** (two easy ones such as a ranking or listicle, one ambitious one such as a case study or scenario).
3. Fill the 3x3 grid: 9 videos.
4. Video 10 doubles down on the best-performing topic-format pair.

### 3-2-1 weekly mix (7 videos per week)
- 2 broader videos (viral potential while still building trust in your expertise)
- 4 narrow videos (designed for conversion)
- 1 chaos agent (pure experiment)

### Content portfolio by obsession level
Plan videos that serve each level of audience obsession (see section 10): high-signal frameworks, belief-shifting transformation stories, and step-by-step action guides with proof.

### Cadence
Expect roughly 50 video reps before traction; plan for zero outliers in the first months; one incremental improvement per video. Posting consistency matters more than any single video.

## 8. Platform Strategy

- **Fish where the fish are:** choose one hero platform where most of your customers spend time, plus email, for six months. Ask existing customers where they consume.
- **Islands, not ecosystems:** each platform is a separate island with different owners and consumption speeds. Do not force cross-platform traffic (links get suppressed; viewers switching from fast to slow consumption churn). The only bridge to build is rented to owned: platform to email list or community. Keep a consistent look and story everywhere ("world building") so discovery feels aligned.
- **Speed mismatch:** short-form feeds run fastest, YouTube slower, email slowest. People move from slow to fast, rarely fast to slow. Sharing a YouTube link on short-form hurts YouTube metrics (no thumbnail click, short watch time); email links work.
- **Audience math (rough equivalence in value):** 10K YouTube subscribers ≈ 25K email subscribers ≈ 50K X followers ≈ 100K Instagram followers ≈ 1M TikTok followers.
- **Where to start:** high-ticket offers that need explanation start on YouTube long-form; low-to-mid-ticket offers that need social proof start on short-form and fund long-form later.
- **Content minutes:** turning a stranger into a buyer takes accumulated minutes with you. Ladder fans from short-form to long-form (YouTube, podcast, livestream).
- **AI era:** AI floods short text and images first, then short video. Add a long-form format and build an intentional edge (unique visuals, delivery, takes, personality, or a rare combination of traits). You lose not because AI content is better, but because oversupply dilutes you.

## 9. Monetization: Value Accrues at the Edges

Three layers: platform (the apps), media (creators), offering (products, services, brands). Most value is captured by the platform and offering layers; little by the media layer, because value capture follows risk. Use media to earn attention, then route it to owned products, services, or uncapped affiliate deals rather than relying on ad revenue or one-off brand deals.

**Rich vs broke viewers (design for the buyer you want)**

| Dimension | Premium buyers prefer | Mass bargain seekers prefer |
|-----------|----------------------|------------------------------|
| Pace | Slower delivery, calmer edits, tasteful visuals | Fast pace, rapid cuts, high stimulation |
| Hook | A specific pain they know they have | A huge outcome in a short time |
| Substance | Expert opinion on a non-obvious insight | Lists of hacks |
| Proof | Verifiable results | Luxury displays |
| Topic | Strategy, psychology, frameworks | One-click tactics |

Mass viewers vastly outnumber premium buyers; optimizing only for views explains "lots of views, no leads".

## 10. Trust, Authority, and Obsession

**Authority types (strongest first):** demonstrated (results you produced for others) > owned (your own results) > borrowed (someone famous proved it). Borrowed authority is acceptable when starting out; sprint to your own proof.

**Three levels of audience obsession (the power of suggestion):** people trust conclusions they reach themselves, so influence indirectly, like a bank shot around their guard.
1. **Signal layer:** consistently higher signal-to-noise than anyone in the category. Every video: relevant, non-obvious, validated.
2. **Belief layer:** change a belief without arguing. Use a contrasting frame (then vs now), a relatable character who started where the viewer is, and a small-effort, large-reward framing.
3. **Action layer:** show quantitative proof plus atomic, implementable steps so the viewer acts and gets a result. Don't command; let them complete the puzzle.

**Five universal inputs for every piece:** relevant and relatable, small effort for big reward (simplify; complexity makes viewers check out), non-obvious (collide two worlds that rarely meet; study adjacent niches), validated with proof (get the proof, then make the content), tactically implementable.

**Social credibility line:** strangers give more chances once an account passes a credibility threshold (on YouTube, roughly 10K subscribers). Reach it by grinding consistently or by a "quality shock": packaging and quality so far above your size that viewers subscribe as a tip of the cap.

## 11. Research and Measurement

**Outlier research (idea mining)**
1. List the top 15-20 accounts in your niche (and adjacent niches).
2. Pull their videos from the last 3-12 months; sort by views and by outlier score (performance relative to that account's average); exclude paid partnerships (very low engagement relative to views usually indicates boosting).
3. Watch top-down. For each, ask: do I have something new and better to say on this topic? If not, skip. The goal is to aim your creativity at validated topics, not to copy.
4. Edu creators can also mine entertainment creators for strong premises that were poorly executed.

**Copy work (skill building):** hand-copy 10-30 top hooks per week, label the hook components, then swap in your own topic. It builds rhythm and phrasing.

**Winners vs losers analysis (monthly)**
1. Filter your own videos to the period since a change you are testing.
2. Sort by views and by your conversion metric (leads per 1,000 views).
3. Compare the top and bottom groups by topic, hook type, format, power words, structure, and CTA attributes. An LLM is good at this pattern finding if you export transcripts and metrics.
4. Keep what wins, drop what loses, test one new variable at a time. CTA attributes alone can move leads per 1,000 views severalfold.

**Production system (about 4 hours per week for 7 short-form videos)**
1. Batch ideation from outliers into a shortlist of 7.
2. Write all 7 scripts in one sitting (about 3 hours); ask "what is the most valuable thing I could share about this for my ideal viewer?"; model hooks loosely on your own past winners. Use AI for research and analysis, not to write the final words, which dilutes voice.
3. Batch record all 7 (about 45 minutes).
4. Hand raw files to editors; post daily.
5. Repurpose top performers into carousels or other formats 30-60 days later.

For long-form, the roles are ideas and packaging, thumbnail design, scripting, recording, and editing. Keep the roles you are best at; hire specialists for the rest rather than a one-stop agency.

**Expectation setting:** the "artist gap" between current skill and taste is large at first; YouTube typically needs about 50 videos (roughly a year weekly) before traction, and ad revenue alone is small even at hundreds of thousands of monthly views. The value is in the business behind the channel.
